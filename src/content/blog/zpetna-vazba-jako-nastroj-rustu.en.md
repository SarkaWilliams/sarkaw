Feedback is one of the most powerful tools a manager or colleague has at their disposal — and at the same time, one of the most commonly misused. The moment it's delivered at the wrong time or in the wrong way, it doesn't support growth — it triggers defensiveness. And defensiveness is exactly the state in which a person learns the least.

## The Difference Between Evaluation and Observation

The most common mistake is confusing a description of observed behavior with a judgment of the person. The sentence "you interrupted your colleague three times in the meeting" describes a specific, verifiable situation. The sentence "you can't listen to others" is already a label placed on the person. The first opens space for a conversation; the second triggers the need to defend oneself. The difference is sometimes just a few words, but it determines whether the feedback gets heard at all.

## Timing Matters More Than We Think

Even precisely worded feedback loses its effect if it arrives at the wrong moment — in front of others, under stress, or long after the situation it concerns. Feedback works best when it's delivered soon after the event, in private, and at a time when the other person has the capacity to actually hear it, not just survive the conversation.

## Feedback as a Dialogue, Not a Monologue

Feedback delivered as a one-way statement often misses its mark, because it leaves no room for context the other person has and you don't. Asking "how did you see that situation" before offering your own assessment changes the whole character of the conversation — from a verdict, it becomes a shared search for what happened and what to do about it.

## How to Receive Feedback Without It Becoming an Attack on Your Self-Worth

Receiving feedback is just as demanding a skill as giving it. It helps to separate information about a specific behavior from information about your worth as a person or professional. Asking "what useful thing can I take from this" instead of jumping to defend yourself opens up space where you can actually work with the feedback, instead of just forgetting it or being hurt by it.

## A Culture Where Feedback Is Routine, Not Exceptional

Where feedback is only given during an annual review or in a moment of crisis, it automatically carries more weight and tension. Where it's a routine part of everyday communication instead — in small, ongoing doses — it loses its dramatic edge and becomes what it's meant to be: an everyday tool for people and teams to gradually improve.

## Feedback as an Expression of Respect

The best feedback doesn't come from a need to be right, but from respect for the other person's capacity to grow. That shift in motivation — from criticism to trust in the ability to change — is often what, in coaching practice, helps managers find a completely different, less conflict-driven way of sharing feedback with their teams.
