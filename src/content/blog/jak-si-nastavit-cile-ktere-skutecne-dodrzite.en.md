At the start of the year, a quarter, or after every important meeting, people make resolutions. A few weeks later, most of them have forgotten those resolutions, and then they blame it on a lack of willpower. But from coaching practice, I know the problem is usually not discipline — it's how the goal was framed from the start.

## A Goal Needs Direction, Not Just a Number

A specific, measurable goal is useful, but it isn't enough on its own. If you don't build a clear connection between the goal and why it actually matters to you, motivation dissolves at the first obstacle. That's why, in coaching, we always come back to the question "what will this actually do for you," until the answer is genuinely yours — not something derived from what's expected of you.

## A Smaller Commitment That Survives an Ordinary Day

Big goals tend to exist only in your head as a picture of an ideal state, with no connection to a specific Tuesday afternoon. It works better when you break a goal down into the smallest possible step you're still willing to take even in a week when nothing goes according to plan. Ambition belongs in the vision — not in next Monday's plan.

## A System Instead of Willpower

Relying on simply "remembering" and "feeling like it" is the most common reason goals disappear into everyday routine. It helps to attach the commitment to something that's already firmly established in your life — a specific day of the week, a routine you already do anyway, or a regular appointment with yourself in your calendar. A goal that depends on mood loses to a goal that depends on structure.

## Feedback Along the Way, Not Just at the End

When the only moment of evaluation is the final result, you lose valuable information about what worked along the way and what didn't. A short, regular check-in — what went well, what I'd do differently next time — lets you adjust the goal as you go, instead of abandoning it halfway because it "no longer fits reality."

## Plan for Deviation

A goal that breaks the first week something doesn't go according to plan was fragile from the start. A more realistic approach expects that you'll occasionally miss a step, and asks how to get back to the plan, instead of treating it as proof that the whole idea was a bad one. A goal's resilience shows precisely in the week when things don't go smoothly.

## A Goal as a Conversation With Yourself

The most lasting goals, in the end, aren't the most ambitious ones, but the ones that emerge from an honest conversation about what you actually want and what you're willing to do for it. In coaching sessions, we often find that clarifying this conversation does work just as important as achieving the goal itself — because it reveals whether the goal even belongs to the person setting it.
