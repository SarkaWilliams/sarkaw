---
title: "Burnout and How to Prevent It in Management Practice"
date: 2026-05-18
excerpt: "Burnout doesn't happen overnight, and it threatens the most responsible managers the most. How to recognize the first signs and act before it's too late."
---

Burnout rarely shows up in management practice as a sudden break. It arrives gradually, through a series of small concessions — I'll just finish this email over the weekend, I can't afford to cancel this meeting, I'll push my vacation to next time. Each individual decision makes sense in the moment. The problem is what they add up to over a year or two.

## Who Is Most at Risk

Paradoxically, the people most at risk aren't those who don't care about their work, but the most responsible ones — managers who feel their team depends on them and therefore won't allow themselves to slow down. It's precisely this high sense of responsibility, combined with the belief that "I have to handle this myself," that creates exactly the conditions in which burnout takes hold most easily.

## Early Signs That Are Easy to Miss

Before the kind of exhaustion that's visible from the outside sets in, subtler changes usually appear first — cynicism toward things that used to feel meaningful, declining patience with people who haven't changed, a sense that even small tasks require disproportionate effort. These signs are easy to explain away as a heavy workload or a demanding period. That's exactly why it's important to notice them before they become a permanent state.

## Rest Isn't a Reward for Performance

In management culture, rest is often understood as something you have to earn first by finishing your work. In reality, it's the other way around — the ability to regularly recover is a precondition for performance working at all in the long run. A manager who only allows themselves to rest occasionally gradually loses exactly the capacity they're trying so hard to protect.

## Boundaries That Protect the Team Too

Setting boundaries isn't just a personal matter — it has a direct impact on the team. A manager who answers messages on the weekend unknowingly sets a standard that gets expected of everyone else too. A clearly communicated boundary — when I'm available and when I'm not — protects both you and the people who take their cues from you.

## What Actually Helps in Practice

The most effective prevention isn't a single switch-off during vacation, but small, regular steps built into everyday operations — real breaks between meetings, space to process decisions instead of reacting instantly, and above all, the willingness to admit exhaustion before it turns into resignation. In this context, coaching often helps as a space where a manager can name these signals out loud, before they grow into something bigger.

## Responsibility That Includes Yourself

A good manager learns to take responsibility not only for the team's results, but for their own capacity to lead them. Preventing burnout, then, isn't a question of weakness or strength, but of long-term sustainability — your own, and that of the whole team, which learns from you how to handle its own limits.
